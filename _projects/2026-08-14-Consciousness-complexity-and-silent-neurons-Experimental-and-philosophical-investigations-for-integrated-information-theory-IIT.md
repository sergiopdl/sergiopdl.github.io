Consciousness is a central part of what it means to be human and thus an undeniable part of the universe. In Chapter 1, we review why it is important to study consciousness, including implications for the treatment of psychiatric disorders and disorders of consciousness, and for the effective administration of general anesthesia. We also review three representative ways of studying consciousness: (1) the neural correlates of consciousness research framework; (2) the development of and adjudication between theories, such as integrated information theory (IIT); and (3) the phenomenological methods of Husserl and Heidegger. Next, we review measures of consciousness, including behavioral measures and measures of neural complexity, such as Lempel-Ziv complexity (LZc) and perturbational complexity index (PCI). PCI, whose development was motivated by IIT to address limitations of LZc, discriminates between levels of consciousness with unprecedented sensitivity and specificity, but less research has examined these measures with conscious contents. A central aim of this dissertation is therefore to investigate whether neural complexity measures that discriminate conscious level can also shed light on conscious contents and on the relationship between conscious level and conscious content. In line with this aim, in Chapters 2 and 3, we present results from a programmatic Registered Report in which we measured participants’ neurophysiological (EEG), subjective, and behavioral responses during normal wakefulness to visual and auditory stimuli varying in the granularity of subjective characteristics, such as meaningfulness. We found that PCI showed the clearest associations with the main visual-granularity manipulation and with subjective ratings, whereas effects in LZc were more measure- and contrast-dependent. These findings suggest that sensory-evoked complexity may be useful for probing conscious contents and that sPCIst may provide a more consistent marker of broad phenomenology-related neural activity than sLZc variants though not necessarily a straightforward readout of phenomenology. In Chapter 4, we provide a philosophical analysis of IIT’s two “silent neuron” predictions, showing how they can be tested, contrary to Bartlett (2022). Drawing on the work of Imre Lakatos, we discuss why theoretical disputes about consciousness are difficult to resolve and provide a framework for concrete progress. This dissertation brings together open questions, presents original experimental and philosophical work bearing on those questions, and shows how theories can inform measures of consciousness, explain discrepancies, and generate testable predictions that can advance the field.
